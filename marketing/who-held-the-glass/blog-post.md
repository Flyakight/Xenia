*A small, sipping-speed history of where the wine world's center of gravity has lived, and how we built a page to tell it.*

Every great wine region has had its moment of holding the glass. Rome, then Bordeaux, then Porto. Later Napa, and later still the Barossa. For two thousand years the center of the wine world has kept moving, pushed along by wars, plagues, trade treaties, a royal wedding and, at one point, one very useful copper spray.

We wanted to see that story all at once. So we drew it.

## The idea

*Who Held the Glass* is an interactive timeline of which wine countries led the world, split by hemisphere. France, Italy, Spain, Portugal and the United States rise and fall across the Northern Hemisphere, from Greek traders planting vines at Marseille around 600 BC to the 2025 transatlantic tariffs. Flip to the Southern Hemisphere and Chile, Argentina, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand take the stage, from the first mission vines in the 1540s to Cloudy Bay and beyond.

Under the lines sit 34 moments that moved the market. Tap one and the story pours into view.

## A few of our favorites

- **146 BC:** With Carthage and Greece conquered, Roman amphora trade makes Italian wine the ancient world's premium export.
- **1152:** Eleanor of Aquitaine marries Henry II. Bordeaux becomes English territory, and the claret trade to London begins.
- **1863:** Phylloxera, an American root aphid, arrives in France and over thirty years destroys most of Europe's vineyards. Spain briefly booms supplying France.
- **1885:** Millardet's spray of copper sulfate and lime, known as Bordeaux mixture, tames downy mildew and helps save European viticulture.
- **1976:** At the Judgment of Paris, California wines beat top French bottles in a blind tasting and put the US on the map.

## Every fact, poured out

History pages have a habit of stating things confidently and citing nothing. We didn't want to do that. Every event on the timeline carries numbered source chips. Tap a number and a small glass card opens with the source, from Britannica and the INAO to the Smithsonian, Wine Australia and the vineyards themselves. Below the chart, a full bibliography we call *the cellar book* lists all 67 sources.

We're also upfront about what isn't measured. The rise-and-fall lines are an editorial estimate of each country's share of wine prestige and trade. There is no consistent market data reaching back to Rome, so read the lines as a story told in shape, not a measurement.

## How it feels

We build digital experiences the way a good dining room works: the details should be felt before they're noticed.

- **Frosted glass.** The chart sits on a milky glass panel, with my hand-drawn vines showing softly through it.
- **Something to pour.** Choosing an event sends a wine drop oozing across the timeline and melting into its marker. The event card fills with a wash of wine as the story settles in.
- **A glass that fills.** A little sketched wine glass fills as you move forward through history.
- **Pencil to paper.** The vines, mushroom and morel are original illustrations, sitting on the same cream paper as the rest of the studio.

It works with a keyboard (the arrow keys step through time), and it respects reduced-motion settings for anyone who'd rather the wine stayed still.
