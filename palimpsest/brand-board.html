ᚁ Beith · birch · the principle

The magic happens when you ask for it

Every glimmer answers something the visitor did, and rewards it with knowledge. The atmosphere waits quietly. The site never performs for its own sake.

The test for every effect

What does the visitor learn from this? If nothing, it goes.

  1. The atmosphere stays quietFog, grain and slow parallax set the mood in the background and never ask for attention.
  2. Glimmers answer an actionHover, tap, focus, or scrolling to something. Nothing sparkles on its own.
  3. Every glimmer reveals somethingA meaning, a source, a story, or a way forward.
  4. Nothing is locked behind magicEverything can be read without interacting. It all works by keyboard, touch and reduced motion.
  5. Once, then restAn effect plays, settles and stays settled. Nothing loops.

Samhain
say "SOW-in" · 31 Oct – 1 Nov

The Gaelic festival at summer's end, when the dark half of the year begins. The boundary between this world and the Otherworld was said to be thinnest.

From Old Irish samain. It appears in the earliest Irish sagas.

púca
say "POO-ka"

A shape-shifting spirit of Irish folklore. It often appears as a dark horse, a goat or a hare. Sometimes mischievous, sometimes helpful, always best treated politely.

The late blackberries are its share of the harvest.

thin place
Irish & Scottish tradition

A place where this world and the other feel close: wells, crossroads, burial mounds, the shoreline. The phrase is modern. The idea is very old.

The Hare

Lepus europaeus · giorria

  • a

    Ears laid back along the body. In Irish and Scottish tales, a hare wounded in the night is found by morning as an old woman with the same wound.

  • b

    Eyes set high and wide. A hare sees almost all the way around itself without turning its head.

  • c

    The long hind leg. The "boxing" of March hares is usually a female fending off a male who is too keen.

Colophon

Palimpsest, a site and theme for authors and practitioners. Demo author: Wren Ashby. Set in IM Fell English, EB Garamond, Caveat and Cutive Mono. The ghost writing under the title is the opening of Pangur Bán, a poem about a scholar and his white cat, written by an Irish monk in the 9th century. The seal reads W and A in Ogham, upward: in the earliest inscriptions Fearn stood for a W sound, and the single cross-stroke is Ailm.
